COUNTING WHAT YOU CAN SEE
An independent re-derivation of the Pakistani long-range SAM holding, and a note on where the published
figure came from

Occasional Paper 2025/06 · South Asia Air and Missile Defence Programme
Published 12 May 2025. Supersedes the figure quoted in our own 2023 backgrounder, which we now withdraw
for the reason given in Section 1.

SUMMARY

The figure "approximately 4 battalions" is quoted almost universally for Pakistan's long-range
surface-to-air holding. We set out to check it and could not. This paper does two things: it traces the
published figure to its origin, and it reports a separate count derived from imagery, arrived at without
reference to the published figure. The two do not agree, and we are not able to reconcile them. Our
position is that the holding is not fewer than three fire units and that the upper bound is unresolved.
We do not endorse "approximately 4 battalions" and we ask that this paper not be cited as if we did.

1. WHERE THE PUBLISHED FIGURE COMES FROM

We identified three outlets carrying "approximately 4 battalions" for the Pakistani holding: a
Jane's-adjacent aggregator, a regional trade blog, and a Quetta-based English-language defence site. In the
ordinary way of things, three outlets agreeing looks like corroboration.

It is not corroboration. All three attribute the figure to the SIPRI Arms Transfers Database. None of the
three states any collection of its own — no imagery, no interview, no document, no independent count. Two
of the three reproduce the surrounding sentence near-verbatim from the earliest of them. What the reader is
looking at is one figure, from one source, printed three times.

Two further things about that one source are worth stating, because they are dropped in the retelling.
SIPRI describes its delivery figures as estimates and says so on the record. And SIPRI's register counts
what was TRANSFERRED, which is a different quantity from what is FIELDED: transferred equipment can be in
training establishments, in depot, written off, or held in reserve, and a register of transfers has no way
to distinguish those from equipment on an operational fire unit. Neither caveat survives into any of the
three secondary versions. Our own 2023 backgrounder repeated the figure with neither caveat, which is why
we are withdrawing it.

We want to be precise about what we are and are not saying. We are not saying the figure is wrong. We are
saying that its being printed three times is not a reason to believe it, and that anyone treating the three
as three looks has miscounted their evidence before they have started counting anything else.

2. WHAT WE COUNTED, AND HOW

We reviewed archived commercial electro-optical imagery over Pakistan for the period 2019 to March 2025,
tasked and untasked, against a published signature description for the HQ-9 family. Our unit of counting
was the PREPARED POSITION: a graded, revetted emplacement whose layout matches the reference signature.
The method, the frame list and the per-position tables are in the appendix, which is not distributed with
the public version of this paper; a summary is below.

We identified six prepared positions of the relevant signature type.

Of those six, three showed PERSISTENT occupancy — equipment present on every usable frame across at least
four passes spanning at least six months. Two showed INTERMITTENT occupancy: equipment present on some
frames and absent on others, with no pattern we could resolve against exercise or maintenance cycles that
we had no access to. One was prepared and never observed occupied on any frame we hold.

3. WHY SIX POSITIONS IS NOT SIX UNITS, AND WHY THREE IS NOT THREE

A prepared position is a place. A fire unit is a formation. The relationship between them is many-to-many
and imagery does not resolve it.

One fire unit routinely holds more than one prepared position — a garrison emplacement and one or more
dispersal or alternate positions is standard practice, and a unit rotating between them will present as
two positions with intermittent occupancy, which is exactly the pattern two of our six show. Conversely one
cantonment can host two units, in which case two positions are two units and not one unit's alternates.

The clearest case in our set is the cantonment at Pano Aqil in northern Sindh, where two prepared positions
about five and a half kilometres apart were both occupied on every pass in the most recent collection.
Order-of-battle references place two long-range air defence regiments at that cantonment. Two positions and
two regiments looks like an answer; it is not one, because no source available to us attaches either
position to either regiment, and a coincidence of counts is not an attribution. The two positions differ in
one observable — the engagement radar planform is not the same at both — and that difference is real, but
a unit converting onto a new fit will also show two planforms, so it does not settle it either. We record
the number of units at that cantonment as OPEN, which is not the same as recording it as two.

We therefore state our result as a floor and not as a figure. Not fewer than three fire units, on the
strength of three persistently occupied positions. Upper bound unresolved, because the three remaining
positions cannot be allocated between additional units, alternate positions of the first three, and
training or depot holdings, and because our imagery coverage of Pakistan is not complete and we have no
way to bound what we have not looked at.

4. THE HONEST COMPARISON

The published figure is approximately four battalions, from one estimate, retold three times, counting
transfers. Our figure is not fewer than three fire units, from our own imagery, counting occupied
positions, with the upper bound open. These are two different quantities measured two different ways and
the fact that "three" and "four" are adjacent numbers should not be mistaken for agreement between them.

If the two are ever to be reconciled it will be by an order-of-battle source that names formations and
attaches them to stations, or by an equipment identifier that ties specific vehicles to specific units.
Neither exists in the open literature for this holding at the time of writing. Until one does, a reader
who needs a number should use ours, should use it as a floor, and should carry the open upper bound with
it every time — a floor quoted without its open bound becomes a figure within about two citations, which
is how the four got where it is.
